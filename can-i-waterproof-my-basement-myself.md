Some of it, yes — and the DIY-able parts are the ones most likely to actually solve the problem, which is not what most people expect.

## Worth Doing Yourself

**Downspout extensions.** Getting roof water six to eight feet away from the wall instead of dumping it at the foundation. A morning's work, under a hundred dollars, and on a real share of houses it is the whole fix.

**Grading.** Building soil up against the foundation so it falls away at roughly an inch per foot for the first six feet. Physical work, cheap materials, genuinely effective.

**Window wells.** Clearing them, adding a cover, checking the drain is not blocked with sixty years of leaves.

**Gutter cleaning.** Unglamorous and frequently the actual cause.

Do all four before paying anyone for anything. If water stops arriving, you have saved five figures.

## Not Worth Doing Yourself

**Interior perimeter drainage.** Cutting a channel around the slab, laying tile at the footing and tying it to a sump is a big, dusty, structural-adjacent job where mistakes are buried under new concrete. Get it done properly.

**Anything on a bowing or horizontally cracked wall.** That is a load problem. [Basement wall repair](/basement-wall-repair/) is engineering, and a strap installed wrongly is worse than no strap because it convinces you the problem is handled.

**Exterior excavation.** Digging a trench to footing depth alongside a loaded foundation wall is genuinely dangerous.

## The One That Wastes Money

Waterproof masonry paint on a wall that has active water behind it. It is sold hard and it fails predictably: hydrostatic pressure pushes the coating off the substrate from behind, usually within a season or two, and you are back where you started having also made the wall harder to diagnose.

It has a legitimate use on a dry wall for damp control and appearance. It is not a fix for water under pressure, and if a wall is [seeping](/water-seeping-through-basement-wall/), pressure is what you have.
